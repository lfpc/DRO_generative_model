\title{Robustness of the SHiP muon shield to parameter tolerances}
\author{}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
How much do the muon hits on the SHiP sensitive plane change when the free parameters of the
muon shield (the magnets M1--M7, including the heights of the magnets and of their yokes; 58 in
all, with the hadron absorber M0 held at nominal) deviate from their nominal values? Every design
is simulated in its own field map, computed with the finite-element model of the iron. The muons come from a generative flow,
each with its momentum and its production point in the target. A neural surrogate of the per-muon
hit probability (an ensemble of four DeepONets, trained with simulations targeted at the muons of
$30$--$60$\,GeV that make most hits) gives the gradient, the Hessian and candidate worst-case
directions, and every conclusion is checked with the full simulation on $10^9$ muons. The nominal
design gives about $1170$ hits per $10^9$ muons (three quarters of the muons that cross the last
plane, a cut explained in Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}; the ratios are unaffected). If every parameter may be off by up to
$\varepsilon$, the worst design found within these tolerances (searched on the surrogate, then
simulated) multiplies the hits by $1.61\pm0.06$ at $\varepsilon=1\%$, $2.61\pm0.09$ at $2\%$,
$23.5\pm0.7$ at $3\%$, $890\pm30$ at $5\%$ and $3800\pm110$ at $10\%$. Up to $\pm2\%$ it narrows
M7's exit and outer yoke, widens its inner yoke and weakens its field; from $\pm3\%$ on it
narrows all of M7's widths and widens M4's inner yoke and entrance, which alone do little but
multiply the effect of M7's narrowing. A single parameter at its $\pm10\%$ limit gives at most
$\times2.84\pm0.10$ (the exit width of M7, narrowed); the heights of the magnets and yokes and the
gaps between magnets matter least, at most $7\%$ each. Random independent deviations are much
milder: harmless at $\pm2\%$, mostly harmless at $\pm5\%$, with a heavy tail at $\pm10\%$.
Bounding the worst case takes sub-percent tolerances on one to two dozen parameters, mostly of
M7, M2, M3 and M4: the allocation found for at most $\times2$ (simulated: $\times1.62$) holds
M7's exit width and outer yoke to $\pm0.5\%$ and M3's field and M7's inner yoke and field to
$\pm0.55\%$, twelve parameters below $\pm1\%$; the one for at most $\times1.5$ (simulated:
$\times1.39$) twenty. The surrogate agrees with the simulator to $5\%$ (median) on the
483 designs simulated, but overestimates the worst designs beyond $\pm1\%$, so the numbers
above are simulated. Part of its error is the field maps themselves: re-meshing the iron for
every design adds a noise of $2$--$3\%$ to the hits of each design, as large as the Poisson
noise of $10^9$ muons.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}
%==============================================================================

\paragraph{The shield.}
The design is the hadron absorber M0 and the magnets M1--M7, with 65 free parameters
(Table~\ref{tab:params}): lengths \texttt{Z\_len} and the gaps \texttt{Z\_gap} before each magnet
(from M1 on), the transverse sizes \texttt{dXIn}, \texttt{dXOut}, the inner gaps \texttt{gapIn},
\texttt{gapOut}, the yoke widths \texttt{dXYokeIn/Out}, the height \texttt{dYIn} of each magnet
and the height \texttt{dY\_yokeIn} of its yoke (their exit values move with them; all in cm), and
the field $B$ of each magnet (the \texttt{NI} column, the target field in tesla, from which the
current is set). M0--M4 are rectangular (exit = entrance) and M5 has no free transverse size.

\paragraph{Parameters analysed.}
The surrogate is trained on all 65 parameters, but the analysis holds the seven of M0 at nominal
and works with the other 58: the absorber is taken as built to specification. Its effects are
small (apart from its field and length, at most $1.3\%$ at its limit), its one-at-a-time
curvatures were the least accurate part of the surrogate, and, left free, the box searches leaned
on it where the surrogate is unreliable. All the results below, the validation included, are for
the 58 parameters of M1--M7.
\paragraph{Fields.}
Each design is simulated in its own field map, computed by the finite-element model of the iron
(\texttt{snoopy}; yokes of diluted steel) in a few tens of seconds per design and cached. The
field follows the iron: the return flux in the yokes, the fringe fields and the saturation are
included, so a change of a yoke or a gap changes the field the muons see. At the nominal design
the core fields are within $10\%$ of their targets (M1--M4 $\approx1.90$\,T for $1.87$--$1.90$,
M0 $1.82$ for $1.99$, M6 $-1.18$ for $-1.32$, M7 $-1.90$ for $-2.03$\,T). The cavern is included.

\paragraph{Tolerances.}
Each parameter may vary by $\pm10\%$ of its nominal value:
\begin{equation}
  \phi_i = \phi_{0,i}\,(1 + 0.1\,u_i), \qquad u_i\in[-1,1].
\end{equation}
$u=0$ is the nominal design and $u_i=\pm1$ puts parameter $i$ alone at its limit. A step of
$\|u\|=1$ is ``one tolerance unit'': as large as one parameter at its limit, but spread over all
of them (RMS change $1.31\%$ per parameter). The corners of the box are at $\|u\|=\sqrt{58}=7.6$.
M6 and M7 have negative fields, so a negative change of their $B$ lowers $|B|$.

\paragraph{Muons and figure of merit.}
Muons are drawn from the generative flow of \texttt{generator\_muons} (the physical flux): the
momentum and charge, then the production point given them. They start where they are produced,
along the target ($z$ from $-2.14$ to $0$\,m before the shield) and around the beam axis. The
figure of merit $Y$ is the number of muons crossing the last sensitive plane per $10^9$ muons. At
the nominal design, six simulator runs on the muons of the analysis give $1114$--$1206$ (each
$\pm35$; the transport is stochastic) and four runs on independent muons $1157$--$1192$, a hit
rate of $1.2\times10^{-6}$; the surrogate gives $1201$. Every simulator number uses $10^9$ muons,
the same ones for all designs of a table, with Poisson errors (the transport is stochastic, so
this holds even on shared muons).

All these counts, the surrogate's training labels included, keep only about three quarters of the
muons that cross the plane. The transport stops a muon at the first $2$\,cm step that reaches
$z\geq91-\mathrm{d}z/2=90.995$\,m, so it ends anywhere in $[90.995,91.015]$\,m, and the hit
selection then also required $|z-91|\leq\mathrm{d}z=0.01$\,m, dropping the muons whose last step
ended beyond $91.010$\,m: $0.015/0.02=75\%$ are kept if the end point is uniform over the step
($104$ of $136$ crossings, $76.5\%$, on $10^8$ muons). Which muons are dropped does not depend on the
design, so every ratio $Y/Y_0$, every worst case and every tolerance holds as it is; the absolute
numbers are $\approx0.75$ of the crossings (nominal $\approx1550$ crossings per $10^9$ muons). The
selection has since been corrected to $z\geq90.995$\,m, the transport's own.

\paragraph{Why a surrogate.}
The transport is stochastic, and at this hit rate the noise of one simulation is large against
the effect of a small change of the design. Fig.~\ref{fig:noise_scan} walks one parameter, the
inner yoke width of M1, from $-20\%$ to $+20\%$ in steps of $1\%$, each design simulated once on
the same $10^9$ muons. The hits jump between neighbouring designs by up to $101$ (RMS of the
differences $45$, as expected from two Poisson errors of $\sqrt{Y}\approx34$), while the trend
underneath moves by $2.3$ hits per $1\%$ at nominal and by about $10\%$ over the whole range;
around a quadratic trend the points scatter with $\chi^2/\text{ndf}=0.84$. A gradient from finite
differences of single simulations would need steps of $\sim15\%$ to see this parameter at one
standard deviation, and 58 parameters, their curvatures and the search for worst cases multiply
that cost. The surrogate gives smooth derivatives and fast evaluations instead, and every
conclusion drawn from it is checked with the simulator on $10^9$ muons.

\paragraph{Changes from the previous analysis.}
The previous analysis had 50 parameters around a different nominal design, uniform fields per
iron block, a fixed M0 and a tapered M4. Here the nominal design is the new one, every design
gets its own field map, M0, the gap before M1 and the yoke heights are free, and M0--M4 are
rectangular; no earlier simulation is reused. M0 is in the training data but held at nominal
in the analysis. The picture is the same in kind (coordinated small
deviations are far worse than single large ones, and a few parameters need sub-percent
tolerances), but with the field maps the transverse sizes of M7, its exit width and yokes, have
become the most sensitive parameters, and the training data now include muons simulated at the
momenta where the physical hits are (Sec.~\ref{sec:surrogate}).

\section{The surrogate}
\label{sec:surrogate}
%==============================================================================

\paragraph{Model.}
The surrogate (LCSO) models the hit probability of each muon $x$ for a design $u$,
\begin{equation}
  p(\text{hit}\mid x,u) = \sigma\!\big(b(u)\cdot t(x) + c\big),
\end{equation}
a DeepONet with a branch network $b$ for the design and a trunk network $t$ for the muon
($p_x$, $p_y$, $p_z$, its production point $x$, $y$, $z$, and charge). The hits $Y(u)$ are the
integral of $p$ over the muons, a smooth function of $u$ with exact derivatives. Four DeepONets
(two hidden layers of 128 units, 32 features) are trained with different seeds for up to
$4\times10^5$ steps and their logits averaged; the average is itself a DeepONet with concatenated
features. A seed whose final validation loss is more than $10\%$ above the median of its siblings
would be replaced by a new one; none was ($0.0311$--$0.0324$). The rule uses only the held-out
training designs, not the references. Errors on surrogate quantities are the standard error of the
four members: the surrogate's own seed-to-seed uncertainty.

\paragraph{Training data.}
The training data are simulated once, in eleven pools (Table~\ref{tab:pools}), and reused for every
epoch; nothing is re-simulated during training. Each pool is a set of designs, each simulated in
its own field map with its own fresh muons from the flow:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Designs.} $u=\delta\,s\,r$, with $s$ a scrambled Sobol point in $[-1,1]^{65}$ and
        $r$ uniform in $(0,1)$, for a region of half-width $\delta$ ($\delta=1$: the $\pm10\%$
        box). The radial factor $r$ concentrates the designs towards the nominal one. The first
        16 designs of each pool are the nominal design itself. The \emph{wide} pool ($\delta=2$)
        puts half of its designs beyond the box, so that the box edges are inside the data.
        \emph{Sparse} designs move only one to $K$ parameters (uniform in $[-1,1]$, the others
        at $0$): the one-at-a-time effects and their pairs, which the radial designs, moving all
        65 at once, hardly contain. In the \emph{faces} pool half of the moved parameters are at
        $\pm1$, where the analysis evaluates them. A last pool repeats the first with M0 held at
        nominal, the setting of the analysis, so that the 58 analysed parameters are covered without
        M0's moves in every design.
  \item \emph{Adaptive.} A first ensemble, trained on the first three pools, was run through the
        analysis; its worst designs within $\pm1$--$10\%$ and those of its three tolerance
        allocations (Sec.~\ref{sec:tolerance}), each with four points on its line from the
        nominal design and 12 perturbations within its box, and the 64 random designs on which
        its members disagreed most, were simulated as a fourth pool (200 designs).
  \item \emph{Muons.} Drawn from the flow at a raised \emph{momentum} temperature $T$ (only the
        momentum part of the flow is tempered; the production point keeps its distribution given
        the momentum), which broadens the momentum spectrum and raises the hit rate from
        $1.2\times10^{-6}$ (the physical flux) to $1.7\times10^{-4}$ ($T=1.5$) and
        $1.9\times10^{-3}$ ($T=2$). This does not bias the model, which learns
        $p(\text{hit}\mid x,u)$ per muon; the temperature only decides where in momentum the
        training data is dense. Selecting muons by their kinematics does not bias it either: the
        \emph{band} pools simulate only muons of $p_z=20$--$80$\,GeV ($T=2.5$), and the
        \emph{window} pools only those of $p_z=30$--$60$\,GeV and $p_t<1.2$\,GeV, at the
        physical spectrum ($T=1$; $2\times10^7$ muons per design, about 30 hits at the nominal
        design).
  \item \emph{Rows.} Every hit and a fraction $f$ of the misses are kept ($f=0.4\%$, and $0.02\%$
        in the band and window pools), a case-control sample; the logit is fitted with the
        offset $-\log f$ of each row's pool, so the model remains one of the full probability.
  \item \emph{Split.} The designs, not the rows, are split: 86\,293 for training and 9588 held
        out for early stopping. The validation references of Sec.~\ref{sec:validation} are
        separate simulations, never used for training.
\end{itemize}
In all, $4.4\times10^{11}$ simulated muons give $1.7\times10^8$ hits and $5.9\times10^8$ rows
($5.3\times10^8$ of them for training).

\paragraph{Why the band and window pools.}
The hits that matter at the physical flux are made by muons of $30$--$75$\,GeV, mostly at
$p_t<1$\,GeV (Sec.~\ref{sec:momentum}), and these respond most to the transverse sizes of M7. At
$T=2$ the hits are dominated by muons above $125$\,GeV, so the muons of the physical hits are
rare in the first four pools; without the band and window pools the surrogate underrated the
narrowing of M7 and was off by about $12\%$ on designs in the box. The window pools put
simulation on these muons, at their physical spectrum, and the sparse and face designs on the
one-at-a-time effects at the box faces, where the analysis reads them (the sparse designs in the
interior alone left the curve of M7's outer yoke too steep at its limit). Down-weighting the
high-momentum rows instead did not help.

\paragraph{Integration over the muons.}
A muon's momentum, azimuth and charge are independent in the flow: $(p_z,p_t)$ is the image of a
2-d standard normal latent. The surrogate's hits, gradient, Hessian and per-momentum splits are
therefore computed on a quadrature grid (latent $\times$ azimuth $\times$ charge,
App.~\ref{app:quadrature}) instead of by sampling muons, with one production point per grid node
drawn from the flow once, from a fixed seed. The grid reproduces a much finer one to
$3\times10^{-4}$ in $Y$ and $1.5\times10^{-3}$ in the derivatives, where $10^9$ sampled muons leave
$1$--$2\%$ of noise. Only the simulator uses sampled muons.

\paragraph{The simulated designs of the analyses.}
A wider check at the physical flux is every design the analyses simulated on $10^9$ muons
(\texttt{validation/flux\_check.py}): those of this analysis (Sec.~\ref{sec:single}--\ref{sec:allocation}),
of the first ensemble's, and of earlier analyses with M0 free or with earlier surrogates (their
designs are kept), 483 designs from near the nominal design to beyond $\times1000$; none was used
for training. Comparing $\HH$, the median error is $4.8\%$ and $69\%$ of the designs agree within
$2\sigma$ ($4.3\%$ and $77\%$ for the 403 the simulator puts below $\times3$). By group: the
one-at-a-time scans $3.5\%$ ($89\%$ within $2\sigma$), the walks $4.8\%$, the worst designs along a
direction $5.4\%$, and the worst designs of the tolerance boxes $13\%$. The largest misses are
designs that a box search pushed to the surrogate's maximum beyond $\pm1\%$, which it
overestimates (Sec.~\ref{sec:box}).

The derivatives are well determined: the four members' gradients at $u=0$ agree with the
ensemble's. How the architecture and training were chosen is summarised in App.~\ref{app:study}.

\paragraph{Noise of the field maps.}
The previous analysis, with uniform fields, reproduced the one-at-a-time curvatures to $12\%$;
here they reach only $31\%$. The field maps explain much of the difference. A
design's map is reproducible (the same design gives the same map, bit for bit), but each
magnet's iron is meshed anew whenever its geometry changes, and the field moves with the mesh:
changing M7's exit width by only $0.2\%$ ($1.4$\,mm) changes the field integrated along $z$
through M7 by $0.07$\,T\,m on average over its aperture and core ($1.3\%$), as much as a $1$--$2\%$
change, while the mean field of the magnet varies smoothly ($-4.4\%$ to $+4.1\%$ over
$\pm10\%$). A weak parameter, M3's yoke height, gives the same jitter for a change of $0.15$\,mm.
The changes stay inside the magnet changed. Simulated with common random numbers (the same
$2\times10^8$ muons and transport seed for every design, so that only the geometry and the field
differ), the hits along these small steps scatter by $2$--$3\%$ per design beyond what the same
steps give in uniform fields, where M3's yoke height has no effect at all. At $10^9$ muons this
design noise is as large as the Poisson noise and does not shrink with more muons; it buries the
small effects of the weak parameters, in the simulated references as in the training data,
while the strong parameters stand well above it. The mesh is set in \texttt{snoopy}'s model
(characteristic length $0.15$, and $0.5$ for setting the current from $B$).

%==============================================================================
\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%==============================================================================

From the surrogate we compute
\begin{itemize}
  \item the gradient $g=\nabla_u Y$ and Hessian $\nabla^2_u Y$ at $u=0$, and the exact
        one-at-a-time change $Y(\pm e_i)/Y_0-1$ of each parameter at its limit;
  \item the active subspace, the eigenvectors of $C=\mathbb{E}_x[\nabla_u p\,\nabla_u p^\top]$:
        the directions in which the hit probability of individual muons changes most;
  \item candidate worst directions, each of unit length:
    \texttt{ascent} ($v\propto g$),
    \texttt{worst} (maximiser of the quadratic model $g\cdot v+\tfrac12 v^\top\nabla^2Y\,v$ on
    $\|v\|=1$),
    \texttt{hess1} (top Hessian eigenvector), \texttt{act1}, \texttt{act2} (top active
    directions), \texttt{corner} ($v\propto\operatorname{sign} g$: every parameter moved by the
    same amount), \texttt{random}, and \texttt{worst2} (as \texttt{worst}, with M7's parameters
    held at nominal: the worst direction that does not involve M7);
  \item 4096 random designs in the box;
  \item the worst design for a given tolerance and a tolerance allocation
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:tolerance}).
\end{itemize}
Each direction is then walked, $u=t\,v$ for $t\in\{-1,-\tfrac12,\tfrac12,1\}$, and simulated.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Single parameters}
\label{sec:single}

43 of the 58 one-at-a-time effects are resolved beyond three times the members' scatter
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}, Table~\ref{tab:oat}). The most sensitive parameters are those of
\emph{M7}: its exit width and the outer yoke at its exit, narrowed by $10\%$, multiply the hits by
$2.8$ and $2.4$ (simulated), and its field lowered by $10\%$ by $1.5$. Next comes the entrance
width of M2 (harmful on both sides: $\times1.6$ narrowed, $\times1.2$ widened), then the lengths
and fields of the upstream magnets M1--M4, each harmful when shortened or weakened ($\times1.2$ to
$1.4$ at $-10\%$), and M7's inner yoke, harmful on both sides ($\times1.16$ and $\times1.24$). Most
responses are one-sided: one side of the tolerance hardly matters, the other is harmful. Per magnet
(Fig.~\ref{fig:magnets}), M7 carries the largest share of the gradient ($79\%$ of its norm), then
M3 and M4 ($34\%$ each), M1 and M2 ($25\%$ each). The \emph{heights} are among the least
sensitive parameters: no magnet height changes the hits by more than $7\%$ at its limit (M7's), no
yoke height by more than $6\%$ (M4's), no gap between magnets by more than $1.1\%$, and none ranks
above 23rd. (Of M0, held at nominal, only the field, $+26\%$ at $-10\%$, and the length,
$+12\%$, matter; its widths, gap and heights change the hits by at most $1.3\%$.)

\paragraph{Simulated.} The surrogate's eight largest were simulated at their worse limit
(Table~\ref{tab:oat}) and in scans at $\pm5\%$ and $\pm10\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:scans}). 26 of the 32
scan points agree within $2\sigma$ and 31 within $3\sigma$; the one-at-a-time designs are where
the surrogate is most accurate ($3.5\%$ median error). At the worse limit it is within $10\%$ of
the simulator for all eight; the largest differences are M7's outer yoke, $\times2.59$ for
$\times2.37\pm0.08$, and M3's field, $\times1.36$ for $\times1.30\pm0.05$ (and $\times1.51\pm0.06$
in the scan, whose own nominal run came out $5\%$ low). The scans miss most at $+5\%$ of the
lengths of M2--M4, where the simulator sees no change and the surrogate $7$--$11\%$ fewer hits.

\subsection{Curvature and active subspace}

Two Hessian eigenvalues stand out (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectra}): $4.89\pm0.54\,Y_0$ per unit$^2$ and
$2.24\,Y_0$, then $0.73$, $0.49$, $0.44$ and $\lesssim0.18\,Y_0$; 22 are negative, the lowest
$-0.26\pm0.33\,Y_0$. Along the gradient the second-order term at one tolerance unit is larger than
the first-order one ($1.29\,Y_0$ vs.\ $0.79\,Y_0$), and along the worst direction four times
as large ($2.42$ vs.\ $0.61\,Y_0$): the response is strongly non-linear at that scale. The top
eigenvector is M7's yokes and exit width: the inner yoke wider, the outer yoke and exit narrower.

The active subspace is effectively one-dimensional ($98.8\%$ of $\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_u p\|^2]$).
Its direction \texttt{act1} shortens M2--M4 by $3.7\%$ and lowers the fields of M1--M4 by
$2.6$--$3.6\%$ together, i.e.\ it lowers the upstream bending power (Table~\ref{tab:dirs}; the
sign of an eigenvector is arbitrary). It is at $\cos=0.46$ with the gradient: individual muons are
deflected most along it, and part of the changes cancels in the total.

\paragraph{Small coordinated changes are far more harmful than a large single one.}
Moving every parameter by at most $5.9\%$ (RMS $1.31\%$) along \texttt{worst} multiplies the hits
by $3.5\pm0.1$, along \texttt{hess1} by $3.6\pm0.1$ (Table~\ref{tab:walks}). The simplest such
pattern, \texttt{corner}, moves \emph{every} parameter by exactly $1.31\%$ with the sign of its
gradient and gives $\times1.58$. Only M7's exit width, alone at $10\%$, comes close
($\times2.8$). A random direction of the same size changes little ($0.99\pm0.04$): the damage
comes from the pattern of signs, not the size of the step.

\paragraph{The surrogate gets the directions and, within one unit, the sizes.}
Its four harmful directions (\texttt{worst}, \texttt{hess1}, \texttt{ascent}, \texttt{act1},
$\times2.6$--$3.6$ simulated) are the simulator's four worst; only the first two swap places, within
their errors. Within one tolerance unit it agrees with the simulator to $2\sigma$ in 28 of the 32
points and to $3\sigma$ in 31 (Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}). It misses most at $t=\tfrac12$ of
\texttt{worst} ($2.09$ vs.\ $1.88\pm0.07$), \texttt{hess1} ($2.07$ vs.\ $1.86\pm0.07$) and
\texttt{act2} ($1.20$ vs.\ $1.05\pm0.04$), all through M7's exit or yokes, which it puts
too early on the rise, and at $t=-1$ of \texttt{worst2} ($2.46$ vs.\ $2.21\pm0.08$).

\paragraph{The quadratic model is not enough.}
Built from the gradient and Hessian, it misses the shape of the harmful directions
(Fig.~\ref{fig:walks}): the hits grow roughly exponentially. The derivatives give the directions;
magnitudes come from the surrogate and the simulator.

\paragraph{The worst direction is M7's yokes; less upstream bending is harmful too.}
\texttt{worst}, \texttt{hess1} and the largest part of \texttt{ascent} widen M7's inner yoke and
narrow its outer yoke and exit width (Table~\ref{tab:dirs}, Fig.~\ref{fig:dirgrid}). They are harmful on one side only
($\times3.5$ and $\times0.93$ along \texttt{worst}): the nominal design sits on the flank of a
valley, not at its bottom (Fig.~\ref{fig:plane}). \texttt{act1}, nearly independent of M7, gives
$\times2.56$ at $t=+1$ (shorter and weaker M1--M4) and $\times1.03$ at $t=-1$.

\paragraph{Without M7 the worst direction is M2--M4, and harmful both ways.}
With M7's parameters held at nominal, the worst direction of the quadratic model,
\texttt{worst2}, widens M2's entrance and narrows and weakens M3 and M4, with M1--M3 shorter
(Table~\ref{tab:dirs}, Fig.~\ref{fig:dirgrid}; cosine $0.24$ with \texttt{worst}). At one
tolerance unit it gives $\times2.47\pm0.09$ simulated (surrogate $2.40\pm0.05$), against
$\times3.55$ for \texttt{worst}: M7 accounts for the worst part, but the rest of the shield is
nearly as sensitive. Unlike \texttt{worst}, it is harmful in both directions ($\times2.21\pm0.08$
at $t=-1$): it runs across the valley of M2's entrance width, which sits at its bottom
(Sec.~\ref{sec:single}). The surrogate overestimates that side ($2.46$, $2.4\sigma$).

\subsection{The worst case for a given tolerance}
\label{sec:tolerance}
\label{sec:box}

\paragraph{Method.} If every parameter may be off by up to $\varepsilon$, the designs allowed
form the box $|u_i|\le\varepsilon/10\%$. Its worst design is found on the surrogate, by
projected-gradient ascent of $\log Y$ over the box from 16 starting points (the sign of the
gradient, the scaled worst direction and random corners; 300 Adam steps each, all batched,
seconds per box), and then simulated on $10^9$ muons. The simulated values are real designs
within the tolerances, so the true worst case is at least as bad; the search is as good as the
surrogate, which is accurate there at $\pm1\%$ and overestimates beyond. The worst direction
of Sec.~\ref{sec:walks} maximises the hits on a sphere ($\|u\|=1$); on a box the maximum is at a
corner, every parameter at a limit with the harmful sign, so scaling the direction down
underestimates it.

\subsection{Tolerance allocation}
\label{sec:allocation}

\paragraph{Method.} Which tolerances keep the worst case below a target? Greedily: start with
every parameter at $\pm10\%$; find the box's worst design $u^*$ as above; tighten the two
parameters that contribute most to it (the largest first-order shares of $\log Y$,
$u^*_i\,\partial\log Y/\partial u_i$) by a factor $0.8$, down to a floor of $\pm0.5\%$; repeat
until the worst case is below the target. The first box that meets each target is its
allocation, and its worst design is simulated. This is a heuristic, not an optimum: other
allocations may loosen some parameters for the same target. It runs on the surrogate, which
overestimates the worst designs beyond $\pm2\%$, so it errs on the tight side.

\subsection{Which muons respond}
\label{sec:momentum}

The muons are split into bins of $p_z$ (and separately $p_t$ and charge), and each bin's hits
$Y_b$ get their own gradient, one-at-a-time effects and worst direction (Fig.~\ref{fig:pz_overview}).
Below $75$\,GeV the per-bin magnitudes carry the surrogate's larger uncertainty there
(Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}), so the directions and signs are the robust part; the simulated hits
per $p_z$ range (Fig.~\ref{fig:simpz}) confirm them.

\paragraph{Three populations.}
Half of the muons have $p_z<30$\,GeV, but at the nominal design the hits come from two groups
(Table~\ref{tab:pz}; simulated shares in parentheses): \emph{intermediate} momenta,
$30$--$75$\,GeV, with $62\%$ of the hits ($64\%$; $58\%$ at $30$--$50$\,GeV alone) at a hit rate
of $1$--$2\times10^{-6}$; and \emph{high} momenta above $125$\,GeV, $1.2\%$ of the muons but
$29\%$ of the hits ($26\%$ above $150$\,GeV), with hit rates up to $2.4\times10^{-3}$ above
$300$\,GeV: the muons the shield does not deflect enough. A third group, \emph{soft} muons of
$20$--$30$\,GeV, gives $6\%$ of the hits ($5\%$) but responds most strongly to some changes.

\subsection{Rediscovering the field integral}
\label{sec:relations}

Can the physics be read off the surrogate without being put in? For muons that the upstream
magnets must sweep out, one expects the field integral: a muon of momentum $p$ crossing a magnet of
field $B$ and length $L$ is bent by $\theta\approx0.3\,BL/p$ (T, m, GeV). The surrogate is not
told this: it sees 58 numbers per design. The analysis below (\texttt{analysis/discover.py})
starts from the surrogate alone and builds the relation in steps, each read off the previous
one. Two objects are used:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{per muon}, at the nominal design: the active subspace of each $p_z$ bin,
        $C_b=\mathbb{E}_{x\in b}[\nabla_up\,\nabla_up^\top]$, which for the DeepONet has the
        closed form $\nabla_up=p(1-p)\,J^\top t(x)$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:surrogate});
  \item \emph{per design}: the gradient of $\log Y_b$ on $1024$ random designs of the whole box
        (all 58 parameters up to $\pm10\%$), in logarithmic coordinates
        $\partial\log Y_b/\partial\log\phi_i$, where products of parameters become sums.
\end{itemize}
Uncertainties are the spread of the four ensemble members.

\paragraph{Reading a direction.}
In logarithmic coordinates a direction is a set of exponents, and the simple ways in which
parameters combine leave recognisable patterns in it (Table~\ref{tab:reading}). At one design
these are hypotheses only: a sum and a product, or a ratio and a difference, have the same
gradient there. They differ in how the exponents change from design to design, and each reading
below is tested that way, on the 1024 designs, with nothing fitted where possible.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering\small
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    hidden variable & exponents at one design & across the designs \\
    \midrule
    product $\phi_i\phi_j$ & equal & equal on every design \\
    ratio $\phi_i/\phi_j$ & equal and opposite & equal and opposite on every design \\
    difference $\phi_i-\phi_j$ & opposite, in proportion $\phi_i:\phi_j$ & the proportion follows $\phi_i/\phi_j$ \\
    sum $\sum_mc_mP_m$ & in proportion to the terms $c_mP_m$ & each term's share follows its own $P_m$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{How a hidden variable shows in the exponents $\partial\log Y/\partial\log\phi$.}
  \label{tab:reading}
\end{table}

\paragraph{1. Every high-momentum muon sees one variable.}
Above $75$\,GeV the first eigenvector of $C_b$ carries $96$--$99.6\%$ of
$\mathbb{E}|\nabla_up|^2$ ($99.6\%$ above $300$\,GeV; Fig.~\ref{fig:discover_variable}a). Not
only the hits of the bin but each muon depends on the design through one and the same
combination of the parameters, whatever its own function of it.

\paragraph{2. The variable is made of field and length of M1--M4.}
Above $300$\,GeV the first direction (Fig.~\ref{fig:discover_variable}b) is concentrated on
eight parameters, the field and the length of M1--M4 (loadings $0.25$--$0.37$, all of one sign:
more field or more length, fewer hits). Next come M7's entrance, field and exit with opposite
sign ($-0.24$, $-0.16$, $-0.14$: a wider or weaker M7 lets more through), and nothing else above
$0.10$; the other 45 parameters carry $4.6\%$ of the direction. Within each magnet, field and
length have nearly the same loading (the smaller over the larger $0.97$, $0.97$, $0.84$ and $0.72$
for M1--M4): by Table~\ref{tab:reading}, a product. On the 1024 designs
$\partial\log Y/\partial\log B_m=\partial\log Y/\partial\log L_m$ holds to $0.4$--$1.1\%$ of the
variance for M1--M3 and $4.5\%$ for M4, whose length counts somewhat more than its field (a
longer magnet also adds iron).

\paragraph{3. Each field pairs with its own magnet's length.}
Equal exponents alone do not say which length goes with which field: all eight are of similar
size, so the field of one magnet and the length of the next are nearly equal too. The pairing
shows across the designs. If the variable is a sum of terms $B_mL_{\sigma(m)}$, field $m$'s share
of the exponent follows its own term's share, $B_mL_{\sigma(m)}/\sum$, with slope one and nothing
fitted. Of the 24 ways of pairing the four fields with the four lengths, the one with each
magnet's own length explains most of the variance of the field shares in every bin above
$175$\,GeV ($0.51$--$0.58$, the next best $0.34$--$0.38$; Fig.~\ref{fig:discover_variable}c).
The variables are $P_m=B_mL_m$.

\paragraph{4. The lever arm.}
How the direction splits between the four magnets (Fig.~\ref{fig:discover_variable}d) is
$0.201$, $0.285$, $0.265$, $0.249$ ($\pm0.003$) for M1--M4. For a sum, these are the shares of
the terms. The plain field integral $B_mL_m/\sum BL$ gives $0.181$, $0.271$, $0.275$, $0.274$
(fields in tesla, lengths in cm): the upstream magnets count more. A kick at $z$ moves the muon at
the sensitive plane by $\theta\,(z_s-z)$, and weighting each magnet by its distance to the plane,
$B_mL_m(z_s-z_m)/\sum$ with $z_s=82$\,m, gives $0.199$, $0.283$, $0.269$, $0.250$, within $0.004$
of the surrogate. Fitted freely, the weights fall to zero at $z_0=76\pm5$\,m, close to the
planes at $82$ and $91$\,m. The variable is the first moment of the field,
$\Xi=\sum_mB_mL_m(z_s-z_m)$: the muon's displacement at the detector, not its angle.

\paragraph{5. The products add up.}
At the nominal design a sum $\sum_mc_mP_m$ and a product $\prod_mP_m^{e_m}$ with $e_m$ equal to
the shares above have the same gradient; they differ in how the shares change across the box. For
the product they are fixed; for the sum they follow each magnet's own $P_m$. On the 1024 designs
the shares follow $P_m$ one for one (Fig.~\ref{fig:discover_action}a). Fitted with the aggregate
$(\sum_mc_mP_m^\rho)^{1/\rho}$, which contains both ($\rho=0$ the product, $\rho=1$ the sum),
$\rho=0.97\pm0.03$ above $300$\,GeV and $1.00\pm0.04$ and $1.00\pm0.06$ in the $225$--$300$ and
$175$--$225$\,GeV bins, with weights $c_m$ that again follow the lever arm ($1.09$, $1.06$,
$0.96$, $0.89$).

\paragraph{6. The collapse.}
Against $\Xi$ the hits of the 1024 designs fall on one curve per bin once the other parameters'
linear contribution is removed (Fig.~\ref{fig:discover_action}b). $\Xi$ alone explains
$81$--$83\%$ of the variance of $\log Y_b$ above $125$\,GeV with all 58 parameters varying, the
best single parameter $14\%$; with the other parameters' linear terms added, $99\%$ above
$175$\,GeV. The curve steepens with momentum: near nominal $Y_b\propto\Xi^{-11}$ at
$125$--$175$\,GeV and $\Xi^{-34}$ above $300$\,GeV, where $1\%$ more field integral removes
$29\%$ of the hits.

\paragraph{7. Simulated.}
Four changes of M1--M4 were simulated on $4\times10^7$ muons with $p_z>125$\,GeV
(Fig.~\ref{fig:discover_action}c). Above $300$\,GeV, $5\%$ more field gives $\times0.20$
(surrogate $\times0.20$) and $5\%$ less length $\times5.1$ (surrogate $\times4.9$). The two
together, which keep $BL$ fixed, give only $\times1.34$, and reversed $\times0.87$ (surrogate
$\times1.24$ and $\times0.83$); what remains is the extra weight of the length. Field and length
of M1--M4 trade one for one for the high-momentum muons, and what a tolerance must hold is their
product.

\paragraph{8. Soft muons: a second variable.}
The same reading at low momentum finds something else (Fig.~\ref{fig:discover_variable}e). At
$10$--$20$\,GeV one direction carries $97\%$ of the muons' gradients and at $20$--$30$\,GeV
$88\%$; it is led by the entrance widths of M2 and M3, with loadings $-0.59$ and $+0.65$
($-0.62$ and $+0.57$; $78\%$ and $71\%$ of the direction), then M3's field and M2's yoke. Equal and
opposite exponents mean the two widths act through one antisymmetric combination: wider M2 and
narrower M3 give more hits, and equal changes of both nearly cancel. M2 is rectangular, so its
entrance is also its exit ($70$\,cm) and M3's entrance ($64$\,cm) is a step inward: the soft
muons see the step between the two apertures. Across the designs the antisymmetry holds to
$7$--$11\%$ of the variance (Fig.~\ref{fig:discover_variable}f). Whether it is the ratio of the
widths or their difference (the step in cm) the surrogate does not resolve: at the nominal
widths the two differ by $9\%$ in the exponents, and the fitted interpolation (exponent of
$d_3/d_2$ in their proportion, $0$ for the ratio, $1$ for the difference) gives $-0.2\pm0.3$ at
$10$--$20$\,GeV and $2.5\pm0.3$ at $20$--$30$\,GeV, where the surrogate is least precise
(Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}). Between $30$ and $75$\,GeV, where most hits are, the entrances,
yokes and fields of M2 and M3 mix ($81$--$88\%$ in the first direction) and no single simple
variable appears; these are the populations of Sec.~\ref{sec:momentum}.

The steps use only the surrogate; the physics enters only to name what they find: a sum over the
magnets of field times length, weighted by the distance to the detector. That a surrogate trained
on 58 numbers per design returns the field integral and its lever arm is a check that it learned
the shield's physics, not only its training points; the relation holds to the extent the surrogate
is right, and the simulation confirms the part tested. The second-order terms of the expansion
carry the same information but are noisier: in the nominal log-Hessian, after the part along the
gradient is removed, each magnet's field-length cross term is $60$--$90\%$ of the value the sum
predicts, but couplings of each field with its own yoke and aperture are as large.

\section{Conclusions}
%==============================================================================

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The design is fragile to coordinated deviations.} With every parameter off by up
        to $\varepsilon$, the worst design found (on the surrogate, then simulated) gives
        $\times1.6$ at $\varepsilon=1\%$, $\times2.6$ at $2\%$, $\times24$ at $3\%$, $\times890$
        at $5\%$ and $\times3800$ at $10\%$; these are lower bounds on the true worst case,
        tight at $1\%$. Moving every parameter by $1.31\%$ with the sign of its gradient
        already gives $\times1.6$.
  \item \textbf{M7's widths are the core of it, with M4's inner yoke.} The worst direction narrows
        M7's outer yoke and exit and widens its inner yoke; these three alone at their scaled
        values give $\times3.5$. The worst box designs from $\pm2\%$ on add M4's inner yoke widened,
        which alone does nothing but with M7 narrowed multiplies the hits by up to seven; with
        M7's widths at nominal such designs are harmless.
  \item \textbf{Bounding the worst case needs sub-percent tolerances on one to two dozen parameters
        and a few percent on most others.} The allocation found for at most $\times2$
        (simulated $\times1.62$) holds M7's exit width and outer yoke to $\pm0.5\%$, M3's field
        and M7's inner yoke and field to $\pm0.55\%$, twelve parameters below $\pm1\%$, median
        $3.7\%$; for at most $\times3$ (simulated $\times1.99$), six below $\pm1\%$, median
        $5.1\%$; for at most $\times1.5$ (simulated $\times1.39$), twenty below $\pm1\%$, median
        $2.6\%$. M7, M2's and M3's entrances, M4's inner yoke and the lengths and fields of M1--M4
        need the tightest tolerances; the gaps between magnets and the heights of magnets and
        yokes the loosest. A target of $\times1.25$ is beyond what the surrogate resolves.
  \item \textbf{Single parameters underestimate the risk.} The worst one at its $\pm10\%$ limit
        (the exit width of M7, narrowed) gives $\times2.8$; M7's outer yoke $\times2.4$, M2's
        entrance $\times1.6$ and M7's field $\times1.5$. The top five changes of the worst
        direction together give $\times5.7$, all 58 $\times7.4$. M7 matters most; the heights
        hardly matter ($\le7\%$ each at $\pm10\%$), nor the gaps between magnets ($\le1.1\%$).
  \item \textbf{Random deviations are much milder than the worst case.} Independent errors of
        $\pm2\%$ keep the hits within about $\pm20\%$; at $\pm5\%$ most designs stay within
        $\pm50\%$, with a tail to $\times1.8$--$3.8$; at $\pm10\%$ the median design is more than
        twice as bad and the tail reaches $\times16$ among eight simulated designs. A
        specification can therefore be much looser if the errors are independent than if it
        must hold against the worst combination.
  \item \textbf{Different muons pull in different directions.} The hits come from muons of
        $30$--$75$\,GeV ($64\%$) and above $150$\,GeV ($26\%$). Narrowing M7 lets through the
        intermediate, low-$p_t$ muons (up to $\times5$ below $0.5$\,GeV); weakening or shortening
        the upstream magnets the high-momentum ones; M2's and M3's entrances the soft ones. The
        worst direction acts on the intermediate and soft muons and leaves the high-momentum ones
        nearly unchanged.
  \item \textbf{The surrogate rediscovers the field integral.} From the surrogate alone, with
        no physics put in: above $75$\,GeV every muon depends on the design through one
        variable ($96$--$99.6\%$ of its gradient), field times length of M1--M4, summed
        ($\rho=0.97\pm0.03$; $0$ would be a product) with each magnet weighted by its distance to
        the sensitive plane, $\sum_mB_mL_m(z_s-z_m)$, to within $0.004$ in the shares. Above
        $300$\,GeV the hits go as its $-34$th power. Simulated: $5\%$ more field gives
        $\times0.20$, $5\%$ less length $\times5.1$, both together only $\times1.34$. A tolerance
        on M1--M4 must hold the product of field and length. Soft muons ($10$--$30$\,GeV) see
        another single variable, the step between the entrances of M2 and M3.
  \item \textbf{The surrogate is quantitative within $\pm1\%$ and on single parameters.} On the
        483 designs the analyses simulated, its median error is $5\%$; it reproduces every
        parameter alone to $10\%$ (31 of 32 scan points within $3\sigma$), the walks within one
        tolerance unit (28 of 32 within $2\sigma$), the worst design of the $\pm1\%$ box to
        $9\%$, and the random designs. It overestimates the combination of M7's widths with
        M4's widths, and so the worst designs beyond $\pm1\%$, by factors of $1.2$ to $50$.
        Every number used for a decision here is simulated.
  \item \textbf{The field maps limit the precision.} Each design's iron is meshed anew, which adds
        $2$--$3\%$ of noise to its hits, as much as the Poisson noise of $10^9$ muons. It hides the
        small effects of the weak parameters (the one-at-a-time curvatures are off by $31\%$ over
        the 58 parameters, $8\%$ over the eight strongest) and caps the tightest tolerance target
        that can be verified at about $\times1.5$. A finer or fixed mesh in the field maps is the
        next step.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The field maps use \texttt{snoopy}'s diluted-steel yokes (its full model fails on M5,
        which has no gap). The height of a magnet, and of its yoke, moves its entrance and exit
        together. Each map is meshed anew, which adds $2$--$3\%$ of noise to every design's hits
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}).
  \item The hadron absorber M0 is held at nominal; its tolerances are not analysed.
  \item Muons come from the generative flow, so the absolute numbers depend on how well the flow
        reproduces the muon spectrum and the production points.
  \item The surrogate's errors are the scatter of its members: they miss a bias the members
        share, such as their overestimate of the worst box designs (Sec.~\ref{sec:box}). The
        curvatures of single parameters on the high-momentum check sample are off by $31\%$
        over the 58 analysed.
  \item The random-tolerance distributions rest on eight simulated designs per level and on the
        surrogate for their tails.
  \item The worst designs within a tolerance are found on the surrogate: their simulated values
        bound the true worst case from below, tightly at $\pm1\%$, loosely beyond. The
        tolerance allocation is a greedy heuristic on the surrogate, not an optimum.
\end{itemize}

\section{Integrating the surrogate over the muons}
\label{app:quadrature}

With $A(z)$ the kinematic envelope of the sampled muons and $N=10^9$,
\begin{equation}
  Y(u) = \frac{N}{\int\!\mathrm{d}^2z\,\mathcal{N}(z)A(z)}\sum_{q=\pm1}P(q)\int\!\mathrm{d}^2z\,
  \mathcal{N}(z)\,A(z)\int_0^{2\pi}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{2\pi}\,
  \mathbb{E}_{r\mid z,q}\,p\big(\text{hit}\mid u, x(z,\varphi,q,r)\big),
\end{equation}
where $z$ is the flow's 2-d momentum latent, $\varphi$ the azimuth (uniform), $q$ the charge (with
the data's $\mu^+$ fraction) and $r$ the production point, which the flow draws given the
momentum and charge. The momentum, azimuth and charge are integrated on a grid: $801^2$ latent
points on $[-7,7]^2$ with Gaussian weights, 64 azimuths and both charges for the derivatives;
$201^2\times64\times2$ points, with the trunk features cached, for everything else. The
production point is drawn once per grid node from a fixed seed, so the grid is a fixed set of
muons; these draws are its only noise.

Against a reference grid of $1601^2\times64\times2$ nodes with four production points each
(Fig.~\ref{fig:quadrature}), the derivative grid is off by $3\times10^{-4}$ in $Y$,
$1.5\times10^{-3}$ in the gradient and $8\times10^{-4}$ in the Hessian, and the cached grid by
$2.0\times10^{-3}$, $7.0\times10^{-3}$ and $3.6\times10^{-3}$ (rms over eight position seeds); four
production points per node would roughly halve the cached grid's errors. $10^9$ sampled muons
scatter by $0.8\%$ in $Y_0$ and $2\%$ above $300$\,GeV, and $10^8$ by $3\%$ in $Y$, $4\%$ in the
gradient and $0.7\%$ in the Hessian. With on-axis muons (the first analysis) the grid was exact to
$10^{-5}$, and quasi-Monte Carlo helped sampling only by a factor two. Importance-sampling the
simulator at a raised temperature does not help either: the hits that matter are at intermediate
momenta, of which a hotter sample holds fewer.

\section{How the surrogate was chosen}
\label{app:study}

The choices come from a study of the surrogates (\texttt{experiments/}) made for the first
analysis (43 parameters, uniform fields, muons on axis) and not repeated here: this analysis uses
the settings it found best. The study used four toy problems with exact answers (a binary outcome
per sample with known probability; 10--43 parameters) and the shield against its reference
designs (Fig.~\ref{fig:study}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Modelling each muon is what makes it work.} A response surface fitted to the same
        simulations without the muons needs about ten times more simulation for the same gradient.
  \item \textbf{Training data.} Many designs with few muons each beat few designs with many; all
        designs at once beat a rolling buffer; the designs must cover the region where the
        derivatives are read. A hotter momentum temperature ($T=2$) is much better than $T=1$ or
        $1.5$, and $T=3$ no better than $T=2$. A tailored training proposal (the provably best one
        for the error on $Y$) would give at most $1.7\times$ the effective training muons of
        $T=2$, so the temperature is kept. Adding designs far from the nominal one helped only
        with an adaptive pool: a pool uniform in the box alone did not lower the box error on the
        nominal muons, with the adaptive pool it fell by a fifth (four seeds each, scored on the
        same references). This analysis therefore uses many $T=2$ designs with $10^6$ muons each,
        a wider pool instead of the uniform one, and an adaptive pool; and, new here, pools of
        only the muons of $20$--$80$\,GeV and of the low-$p_t$ window at $30$--$60$\,GeV, with
        sparse and face designs (Sec.~\ref{sec:surrogate}).
  \item \textbf{Architecture.} The DeepONet is the most reliable for the hit rate. At the final
        scale ($2\times10^8$ rows) capacity matters (128 units, 32 features), and averaging seeds
        removes most of the scatter of the gradient. Reduced Taylor surrogates (a per-muon
        quadratic on a low-dimensional subspace), the best Hessians on the toys, are worse here
        (one-at-a-time secants $6$--$10\%$, curvatures $18$--$25\%$). An MLP on $[u,x]$ predicts
        somewhat better (box $11\%$ instead of $16\%$, curvatures $10\%$ instead of $12\%$) and
        agrees with the DeepONet's gradient, top eigenvector and worst direction to
        $\cos=0.99$, so the conclusions do not depend on the choice; the DeepONet is kept because
        its separable form allows cached trunk features and exact derivatives.
\end{itemize}
