\title{Robustness of the SHiP muon shield to parameter tolerances}
\author{}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
How much do the muon hits on the SHiP sensitive plane change when the 65 free parameters of the
muon shield (the hadron absorber M0 and the magnets M1--M7, including the heights of the magnets
and of their yokes) deviate from their nominal values? Every design is simulated in its own field
map, computed with the finite-element model of the iron. The muons come from a generative flow,
each with its momentum and its production point in the target. A neural surrogate of the per-muon
hit probability (an ensemble of four DeepONets) gives the gradient, the Hessian and candidate
worst-case directions, and every conclusion is checked with the full simulation on $10^9$ muons.
The nominal design gives about $1200$ hits per $10^9$ muons. If every parameter may be off by up
to $\varepsilon$, the worst design found within these tolerances (searched on the surrogate, then
simulated) multiplies the hits by $1.60\pm0.06$ at $\varepsilon=1\%$, $3.6\pm0.1$ at $2\%$,
$9.2\pm0.3$ at $3\%$ and $640\pm20$ at $5\%$ (hence at least as much at $10\%$). A single
parameter at its $\pm10\%$ limit gives at most $\times2.9\pm0.1$ (the exit width of M7, narrowed);
the heights of the magnets and yokes, the gaps between magnets and most of M0 matter least, at
most $8\%$ each. Random independent deviations are much milder: harmless at $\pm2\%$, mostly
harmless at $\pm5\%$, with a heavy tail at $\pm10\%$. Bounding the worst case takes sub-percent
tolerances on about fifteen parameters, mostly of M7, M2, M3 and M4: the
allocation found for at most $\times2$ (simulated: $\times1.59$) holds M2's entrance width, M3's
field and M7's field, exit width and outer yoke to $\pm0.5\%$. The surrogate is reliable near the
nominal design but not far from it: it misses the full effect of narrowing M7 and overestimates
the worst designs beyond $\pm2\%$, so the numbers above are simulated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup}
%==============================================================================

\paragraph{The shield.}
The design is the hadron absorber M0 and the magnets M1--M7, with 65 free parameters
(Table~\ref{tab:params}): lengths \texttt{Z\_len} and the gaps \texttt{Z\_gap} before each magnet
(from M1 on), the transverse sizes \texttt{dXIn}, \texttt{dXOut}, the inner gaps \texttt{gapIn},
\texttt{gapOut}, the yoke widths \texttt{dXYokeIn/Out}, the height \texttt{dYIn} of each magnet
and the height \texttt{dY\_yokeIn} of its yoke (their exit values move with them; all in cm), and
the field $B$ of each magnet (the \texttt{NI} column, the target field in tesla, from which the
current is set). M0--M4 are rectangular (exit = entrance) and M5 has no free transverse size.

\paragraph{Fields.}
Each design is simulated in its own field map, computed by the finite-element model of the iron
(\texttt{snoopy}; yokes of diluted steel) in a few tens of seconds per design and cached. The
field follows the iron: the return flux in the yokes, the fringe fields and the saturation are
included, so a change of a yoke or a gap changes the field the muons see. At the nominal design
the core fields are within $10\%$ of their targets (M1--M4 $\approx1.90$\,T for $1.87$--$1.90$,
M0 $1.82$ for $1.99$, M6 $-1.18$ for $-1.32$, M7 $-1.90$ for $-2.03$\,T). The cavern is included.

\paragraph{Tolerances.}
Each parameter may vary by $\pm10\%$ of its nominal value:
\begin{equation}
  \phi_i = \phi_{0,i}\,(1 + 0.1\,u_i), \qquad u_i\in[-1,1].
\end{equation}
$u=0$ is the nominal design and $u_i=\pm1$ puts parameter $i$ alone at its limit. A step of
$\|u\|=1$ is ``one tolerance unit'': as large as one parameter at its limit, but spread over all
of them (RMS change $1.24\%$ per parameter). The corners of the box are at $\|u\|=\sqrt{65}=8.1$.
M6 and M7 have negative fields, so a negative change of their $B$ lowers $|B|$.

\paragraph{Muons and figure of merit.}
Muons are drawn from the generative flow of \texttt{generator\_muons} (the physical flux): the
momentum and charge, then the production point given them. They start where they are produced,
along the target ($z$ from $-2.14$ to $0$\,m before the shield) and around the beam axis. The
figure of merit $Y$ is the number of muons crossing the last sensitive plane per $10^9$ muons. At
the nominal design, five simulator runs on the muons of the analysis give $1119$--$1207$ (each
$\pm35$; the transport is stochastic) and four runs on independent muons $1157$--$1192$, a hit
rate of $1.2\times10^{-6}$; the surrogate gives $1229$. Every simulator number uses $10^9$ muons,
the same ones for all designs of a table, with Poisson errors (the transport is stochastic, so
this holds even on shared muons).

\paragraph{Changes from the previous analysis.}
The previous analysis had 50 parameters around a different nominal design, uniform fields per
iron block, a fixed M0 and a tapered M4. Here the nominal design is the new one, every design
gets its own field map, M0, the gap before M1 and the yoke heights are free, and M0--M4 are
rectangular; no earlier simulation is reused. The picture is the same in kind (coordinated small
deviations are far worse than single large ones, and a few parameters need sub-percent
tolerances), but with the field maps the transverse sizes of M7, its exit width and yokes, have
become the most sensitive parameters.

\section{The surrogate}
%==============================================================================

\paragraph{Model.}
The surrogate (LCSO) models the hit probability of each muon $x$ for a design $u$,
\begin{equation}
  p(\text{hit}\mid x,u) = \sigma\!\big(b(u)\cdot t(x) + c\big),
\end{equation}
a DeepONet with a branch network $b$ for the design and a trunk network $t$ for the muon
($p_x$, $p_y$, $p_z$, its production point $x$, $y$, $z$, and charge). The hits $Y(u)$ are the
integral of $p$ over the muons, a smooth function of $u$ with exact derivatives. Four DeepONets
(two hidden layers of 128 units, 32 features) are trained with different seeds and their logits
averaged; the average is itself a DeepONet with concatenated features. A seed whose final
validation loss is more than $10\%$ above the median of its siblings would be replaced by a new
one; none was ($0.0285$--$0.0292$). The rule uses only the held-out training designs, not the
references. Errors on surrogate quantities are the standard error of the four members: the
surrogate's own seed-to-seed uncertainty.

\paragraph{Training data.}
The training data are simulated once, in four pools (Table~\ref{tab:pools}), and reused for every
epoch; nothing is re-simulated during training. Each pool is a set of designs, each simulated in
its own field map with its own fresh muons from the flow:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Designs.} $u=\delta\,s\,r$, with $s$ a scrambled Sobol point in $[-1,1]^{65}$ and
        $r$ uniform in $(0,1)$, for a region of half-width $\delta$ ($\delta=1$: the $\pm10\%$
        box). The radial factor $r$ concentrates the designs towards the nominal one. The first
        16 designs of each pool are the nominal design itself. The \emph{wide} pool ($\delta=2$)
        puts half of its designs beyond the box, so that the box edges are inside the data.
  \item \emph{Adaptive.} A first ensemble, trained on the other three pools, was run through the
        analysis; its worst designs within $\pm1$--$10\%$ and those of its three tolerance
        allocations (Sec.~\ref{sec:tolerance}), each with four points on its line from the
        nominal design and 12 perturbations within its box, and the 64 random designs on which
        its members disagreed most, were simulated as a fourth pool (200 designs).
  \item \emph{Muons.} Drawn from the flow at a raised \emph{momentum} temperature $T$ (only the
        momentum part of the flow is tempered; the production point keeps its distribution given
        the momentum), which broadens the momentum spectrum and raises the hit rate from
        $1.2\times10^{-6}$ (the physical flux) to $1.7\times10^{-4}$ ($T=1.5$) and
        $1.9\times10^{-3}$ ($T=2$). This does not bias the model, which learns
        $p(\text{hit}\mid x,u)$ per muon; the temperature only decides where in momentum the
        training data is dense.
  \item \emph{Rows.} Every hit and $0.4\%$ of the misses are kept, a case-control sample; the
        logit is fitted with the offset $\log 250$, so the model remains one of the full
        probability.
  \item \emph{Split.} The designs, not the rows, are split: 37\,093 for training and 4122 held
        out for early stopping. The validation references of Sec.~\ref{sec:validation} are
        separate simulations, never used for training.
\end{itemize}
In all, $5.2\times10^{10}$ simulated muons give $8.9\times10^7$ hits and $3.0\times10^8$ rows.
At the physical flux, as many hits would need $7\times10^{13}$ muons, i.e.\ about $150\,000$
designs of $5\times10^8$ muons; the same number of simulated muons would buy only $\sim100$ such
designs.

\paragraph{Integration over the muons.}
A muon's momentum, azimuth and charge are independent in the flow: $(p_z,p_t)$ is the image of a
2-d standard normal latent. The surrogate's hits, gradient, Hessian and per-momentum splits are
therefore computed on a quadrature grid (latent $\times$ azimuth $\times$ charge,
App.~\ref{app:quadrature}) instead of by sampling muons, with one production point per grid node
drawn from the flow once, from a fixed seed. The grid reproduces a much finer one to
$3\times10^{-4}$ in $Y$ and $1\times10^{-3}$ in the derivatives, where $10^9$ sampled muons leave
$1$--$2\%$ of noise. Only the simulator uses sampled muons.

On the check sample (hotter momenta) the surrogate reproduces the
one-at-a-time slopes to $9\%$ ($\cos=0.997$) and the designs in the box to $4\%$, but it is $7\%$
high at the nominal design. On the physical flux it is $4\%$ high at the nominal design and $7\%$
and $12\%$ off in the half box and the box. The largest misses are the designs with the most
hits: one box design that the simulator puts at $\times670$ is predicted four times too low. The
hits that matter at the physical flux come from muons of $20$--$50$\,GeV
(Sec.~\ref{sec:momentum}), which are rare in every training sample, and the one-at-a-time designs
of the check sample do not test them. The simulated scans of Sec.~\ref{sec:single} do: there the
surrogate misses the narrowing of M7 and an effect of M4's yoke that the simulator does not show.
Within one tolerance unit the walks of Sec.~\ref{sec:walks} agree with the simulator to $2\sigma$
in 19 of 28 points and to $3\sigma$ in 22, with the surrogate mostly low.

The derivatives are well determined: the four members' gradients at $u=0$ agree with the
ensemble's. How the architecture and training were chosen is summarised in App.~\ref{app:study}.

\section{Method}
%==============================================================================

From the surrogate we compute
\begin{itemize}
  \item the gradient $g=\nabla_u Y$ and Hessian $\nabla^2_u Y$ at $u=0$, and the exact
        one-at-a-time change $Y(\pm e_i)/Y_0-1$ of each parameter at its limit;
  \item the active subspace, the eigenvectors of $C=\mathbb{E}_x[\nabla_u p\,\nabla_u p^\top]$:
        the directions in which the hit probability of individual muons changes most;
  \item candidate worst directions, each of unit length:
    \texttt{ascent} ($v\propto g$),
    \texttt{worst} (maximiser of the quadratic model $g\cdot v+\tfrac12 v^\top\nabla^2Y\,v$ on
    $\|v\|=1$),
    \texttt{hess1} (top Hessian eigenvector), \texttt{act1}, \texttt{act2} (top active
    directions), \texttt{corner} ($v\propto\operatorname{sign} g$: every parameter moved by the
    same amount) and \texttt{random};
  \item the worst corner of the box, by projected-gradient ascent (\texttt{pga});
  \item 4096 random designs in the box;
  \item the worst design for a given tolerance and a tolerance allocation
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:tolerance}).
\end{itemize}
Each direction is then walked, $u=t\,v$ for $t\in\{-1,-\tfrac12,\tfrac12,1\}$ (for \texttt{pga},
up to the box face), and simulated.

\subsection{Single parameters}
\label{sec:single}

43 of the 65 one-at-a-time effects are resolved beyond three times the members' scatter
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}, Table~\ref{tab:oat}). The most sensitive parameters are those of
\emph{M7}: its exit width and the outer yoke at its exit, narrowed by $10\%$, multiply the hits by
$2.9$ and $2.2$ (simulated), and its field lowered by $10\%$ by $1.5$. Next come the entrance
width of M2 (harmful on both sides: $\times1.7$ narrowed, $\times1.3$ widened), then the lengths
and fields of the upstream magnets M0--M4, each harmful when shortened or weakened ($\times1.2$ to
$1.4$ at $-10\%$). Most responses are one-sided: one side of the tolerance hardly matters, the
other is harmful. Per magnet (Fig.~\ref{fig:magnets}), M7 carries the largest share of the
gradient ($73\%$ of its norm), then M4 ($36\%$), M3 ($33\%$), M0 and M2 ($27\%$ each) and M1
($23\%$). The \emph{heights} are among the least sensitive parameters: no magnet height changes
the hits by more than $6\%$ at its limit (M7's), no yoke height by more than $8\%$ (M4's), no gap
between magnets by more than $7\%$, and none ranks above 24th. Of M0 only the field ($+28\%$ at
$-10\%$) and the length ($+13\%$) matter; its widths, gap and heights change the hits by at most
$4\%$.

\paragraph{Simulated.} The surrogate's eight largest were simulated at their worse limit
(Table~\ref{tab:oat}) and in scans at $\pm5\%$ and $\pm10\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:scans}). The fields
and lengths agree within $1$--$2\sigma$ at every point. The transverse sizes do not: the
narrowed exit width and outer yoke of M7 give $\times2.9$ and $\times2.2$ where the surrogate
predicts $\times1.7$, and the narrowed entrance of M2 $\times1.7$ for $\times1.5$; and the
surrogate's top parameter, M4's inner yoke widened ($\times1.69$), has no effect in the simulator
($\times1.01\pm0.04$; the training pools show none either, out to $\pm20\%$). The simulated ranking
is therefore M7's exit width, M7's outer yoke, M2's entrance width, M7's field, then M3's field
and the lengths of M2 and M3.

\subsection{Curvature and active subspace}

Two Hessian eigenvalues stand out (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectra}): $2.79\pm0.13\,Y_0$ per unit$^2$ and
$1.64\,Y_0$, then $0.58$, $0.37$ and $\lesssim0.21\,Y_0$; 24 are negative, the lowest
$-1.7\pm0.8\,Y_0$. Along the gradient the second-order term at one tolerance unit is close to the
first-order one ($0.68\,Y_0$ vs.\ $0.84\,Y_0$), and along the worst direction twice as large
($1.33$ vs.\ $0.66\,Y_0$): the response is strongly non-linear at that scale.

The active subspace is effectively one-dimensional ($98.9\%$ of $\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_u p\|^2]$).
Its direction \texttt{act1} shortens M2--M4 by $3.5\%$ and lowers the fields of M0 and M2--M4 by
$2.6$--$3.4\%$ together, i.e.\ it lowers the upstream bending power (Table~\ref{tab:dirs}; the
sign of an eigenvector is arbitrary). It is at $\cos=0.51$ with the gradient: individual muons are
deflected most along it, and part of the changes cancels in the total.

\paragraph{Small coordinated changes are far more harmful than a large single one.}
Moving every parameter by at most $4.3\%$ (RMS $1.24\%$) along \texttt{worst} multiplies the hits
by $3.6\pm0.1$ (Table~\ref{tab:walks}). The simplest such pattern, \texttt{corner}, moves
\emph{every} parameter by exactly $1.24\%$ with the sign of its gradient and gives $\times1.8$.
The worst single parameter needs a $10\%$ error for a similar effect. A random direction of the
same size changes little ($0.90\pm0.04$): the damage comes from the pattern of signs, not the size
of the step.

\paragraph{The surrogate gets the directions; the sizes up to $\sim20\%$.}
Its best direction, \texttt{worst}, is also the simulator's, and its four harmful directions
(\texttt{worst}, \texttt{hess1}, \texttt{ascent}, \texttt{act1}, $\times2.7$--$3.6$ simulated) are
the simulator's four worst. Within one tolerance unit it agrees with the simulator to $2\sigma$ in
19 of the 28 points (Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}). It misses most along \texttt{act2}, which

\subsection{The worst case for a given tolerance}
\label{sec:tolerance}
\label{sec:box}

\paragraph{Method.} If every parameter may be off by up to $\varepsilon$, the designs allowed
form the box $|u_i|\le\varepsilon/10\%$. Its worst design is found on the surrogate, by
projected-gradient ascent of $\log Y$ over the box from 16 starting points (the sign of the
gradient, the scaled worst direction and random corners; 300 Adam steps each, all batched,
seconds per box), and then simulated on $10^9$ muons. The simulated values are real designs
within the tolerances, so the true worst case is at least as bad; the search is as good as the
surrogate, which is accurate there up to $\pm2\%$ and overestimates beyond. The worst direction
of Sec.~\ref{sec:walks} maximises the hits on a sphere ($\|u\|=1$); on a box the maximum is at a
corner, every parameter at a limit with the harmful sign, so scaling the direction down
underestimates it.

\subsection{Tolerance allocation}
\label{sec:allocation}

\paragraph{Method.} Which tolerances keep the worst case below a target? Greedily: start with
every parameter at $\pm10\%$; find the box's worst design $u^*$ as above; tighten the two
parameters that contribute most to it (the largest first-order shares of $\log Y$,
$u^*_i\,\partial\log Y/\partial u_i$) by a factor $0.8$, down to a floor of $\pm0.5\%$; repeat
until the worst case is below the target. The first box that meets each target is its
allocation, and its worst design is simulated. This is a heuristic, not an optimum: other
allocations may loosen some parameters for the same target. It runs on the surrogate, so it
also tightens M4's inner yoke, which the simulator does not require.

\subsection{Which muons respond}
\label{sec:momentum}

The muons are split into bins of $p_z$ (and separately $p_t$ and charge), and each bin's hits
$Y_b$ get their own gradient, one-at-a-time effects and worst direction (Fig.~\ref{fig:pz_overview}).
Below $75$\,GeV the per-bin magnitudes carry the surrogate's larger uncertainty there
(Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}), so the directions and signs are the robust part; the simulated hits
per $p_z$ range (Fig.~\ref{fig:simpz}) confirm them.

\paragraph{Three populations.}
Half of the muons have $p_z<30$\,GeV, but at the nominal design the hits come from two groups
(Table~\ref{tab:pz}; simulated shares in parentheses): \emph{intermediate} momenta,
$30$--$75$\,GeV, with $63\%$ of the hits ($64\%$; $58\%$ at $30$--$50$\,GeV alone) at a hit rate

$300$\,GeV: the muons the shield does not deflect enough. A third group, \emph{soft} muons of
$20$--$30$\,GeV, gives $6\%$ of the hits ($7\%$) but responds most strongly to some changes.

        high-momentum hits fall ($307\to129$);
  \item random $\pm10\%$ designs can open any group: the worst of the eight ($\times7$) raises
        the $40$--$50$\,GeV hits from $341$ to $6262$, another ($\times3.8$) those above
        $150$\,GeV from $307$ to $3268$, and another ($\times2.0$) both the soft ($84\to362$)
        and the high-momentum ($307\to1196$) ones.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Which parameters act on which muons} (Fig.~\ref{fig:pz_sensitivity}).
The high-momentum hits are controlled by the \emph{upstream} magnets: the lengths and fields of
M0--M4, each changing the hits above $300$\,GeV by $90$--$160\%$ at its worse limit. The soft hits
respond most to the entrance widths of M2 and M3 (up to $\times6$ at $10$--$20$\,GeV, $\times4.5$
at $20$--$30$\,GeV) and the field of M3. The intermediate hits are controlled by the field of M7
($+91\%$ of the $30$--$45$\,GeV hits, $+103\%$ of the $45$--$75$\,GeV ones) and, at
$45$--$75$\,GeV, by M2's entrance width ($\times3.4$). This explains \texttt{act1}: weakening
M0--M4 together lets more high-momentum muons through.

\paragraph{Transverse momentum and charge.}
The sensitivity falls with $p_t$ ($1.44$ per tolerance unit below $0.25$\,GeV, $0.66$--$0.72$
above $1$\,GeV), and the bins below $1$\,GeV want the same changes ($\cos\ge0.94$,
Fig.~\ref{fig:pt}). The muons above $1.5$\,GeV ($1.6\%$ of the muons, $28\%$ of the hits) want
other changes ($\cos=0.05$--$0.23$ with the bins below $1$\,GeV). The narrowing of M7 acts on the
low-$p_t$ muons: its exit width and outer yoke at their worse limit raise the hits below
$0.5$\,GeV by $120$--$140\%$ and those above $1.5$\,GeV by $2\%$. Positive muons give $64\%$ of
the hits; both charges respond to similar changes ($\cos=0.86$), the $\mu^-$ twice as strongly to
M2's entrance width ($+77\%$ vs.\ $+40\%$).

\section{Conclusions}
%==============================================================================

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The design is fragile to coordinated deviations.} With every parameter off by up
        to $\varepsilon$, the worst design found (on the surrogate, then simulated) gives
        $\times1.6$ at $\varepsilon=1\%$, $\times3.6$ at $2\%$, $\times9.2$ at $3\%$ and
        $\times641$ at $5\%$, hence at least $\times641$ at $10\%$; these are lower bounds on
        the true worst case, tight up to $2\%$. Moving every parameter by $1.24\%$ with the sign
        of its gradient already gives $\times1.8$.
  \item \textbf{Bounding the worst case needs sub-percent tolerances on about fifteen parameters
        and a few percent on all the others.} The allocation found for at most $\times2$
        (simulated $\times1.59$) holds M2's entrance width, M3's field and M7's field, exit
        width and outer yoke to $\pm0.5\%$, fifteen parameters below $\pm1\%$, median $2.6\%$;
        for at most $\times3$ (simulated $\times1.93$), nine below $\pm1\%$, median $4\%$. No
        parameter may stay at $\pm10\%$. M7, M2's entrance and the lengths and fields of M0--M4
        need the tightest tolerances; the gaps between magnets, the heights of magnets and yokes
        and M0's transverse sizes the loosest.
  \item \textbf{Single parameters underestimate the risk.} The worst one at its $\pm10\%$ limit
        (the exit width of M7, narrowed) gives $\times2.9$; M7's outer yoke $\times2.2$, M2's
        entrance $\times1.7$ and M7's field $\times1.5$. The top five changes of the worst
        direction together give $\times4$, the top ten $\times6$, all 65 $\times13$: the factors
        multiply. M7 matters most; the heights hardly matter ($\le8\%$ each at $\pm10\%$).
  \item \textbf{Random deviations are much milder than the worst case.} Independent errors of
        $\pm2\%$ keep the hits within about $\pm15\%$; at $\pm5\%$ most designs stay within
        $\pm50\%$, with a tail to $\times2$--$3$; at $\pm10\%$ the median design is more than
        twice as bad and the tail reaches $\times7$ among eight simulated designs. A
        specification can therefore be much looser if the errors are independent than if it
        must hold against the worst combination.
  \item \textbf{Different muons pull in different directions.} The hits come from muons of
        $30$--$75$\,GeV ($64\%$) and above $150$\,GeV ($26\%$). The worst direction raises the
        soft, $30$--$40$\,GeV and high-momentum hits; its opposite, as harmful, raises the
        $40$--$60$\,GeV hits while the others fall. Weakening the upstream magnets lets the
        high-momentum muons through; narrowing M7 the low-$p_t$ ones.
  \item \textbf{The surrogate is quantitative near the nominal design, not far from it.} It
        reproduces the fields and lengths one at a time, the walks within one tolerance unit to
        $\sim2\sigma$ and the worst designs within $\pm1$--$2\%$ to $3$--$10\%$, and it finds the
        harmful directions. But it underrates the narrowing of M7 ($\times1.7$ for $\times2.9$),
        invents an effect of M4's inner yoke, is $12\%$ off on designs in the box and
        overestimates the worst designs beyond $\pm2\%$ by factors of $3$ to $10^4$. Every number
        used for a decision here is simulated.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The field maps use \texttt{snoopy}'s diluted-steel yokes (its full model fails on M5,
        which has no gap). The height of a magnet, and of its yoke, moves its entrance and exit
        together.
  \item Muons come from the generative flow, so the absolute numbers depend on how well the flow
        reproduces the muon spectrum and the production points.
  \item The surrogate's errors are the scatter of its members: they miss a bias the members
        share, such as the M4 yoke and the M7 widths (Sec.~\ref{sec:single}) or the designs far
        from the nominal one (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}). The muons of $20$--$50$\,GeV that make
        most hits are sparse in the training data, and the one-at-a-time references, on the
        high-momentum check sample, do not test them.
  \item The random-tolerance distributions rest on eight simulated designs per level and on the
        surrogate for their tails.
  \item The worst designs within a tolerance are found on the surrogate: their simulated values
        bound the true worst case from below, tightly up to $\pm2\%$, loosely beyond, and at
        $\pm10\%$ the search failed. The tolerance allocation is a greedy heuristic on the
        surrogate, not an optimum, and tightens M4's inner yoke needlessly.
\end{itemize}

\section{Integrating the surrogate over the muons}
\label{app:quadrature}

With $A(z)$ the kinematic envelope of the sampled muons and $N=10^9$,
\begin{equation}
  Y(u) = \frac{N}{\int\!\mathrm{d}^2z\,\mathcal{N}(z)A(z)}\sum_{q=\pm1}P(q)\int\!\mathrm{d}^2z\,
  \mathcal{N}(z)\,A(z)\int_0^{2\pi}\!\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{2\pi}\,
  \mathbb{E}_{r\mid z,q}\,p\big(\text{hit}\mid u, x(z,\varphi,q,r)\big),
\end{equation}
where $z$ is the flow's 2-d momentum latent, $\varphi$ the azimuth (uniform), $q$ the charge (with
the data's $\mu^+$ fraction) and $r$ the production point, which the flow draws given the
momentum and charge. The momentum, azimuth and charge are integrated on a grid: $801^2$ latent
points on $[-7,7]^2$ with Gaussian weights, 64 azimuths and both charges for the derivatives;
$201^2\times64\times2$ points, with the trunk features cached, for everything else. The
production point is drawn once per grid node from a fixed seed, so the grid is a fixed set of
muons; these draws are its only noise.

Against a reference grid of $1601^2\times64\times2$ nodes with four production points each
(Fig.~\ref{fig:quadrature}), the derivative grid is off by $3\times10^{-4}$ in $Y$,
$1.1\times10^{-3}$ in the gradient and $7\times10^{-4}$ in the Hessian, and the cached grid by
$1.7\times10^{-3}$, $5.8\times10^{-3}$ and $3.6\times10^{-3}$ (rms over eight position seeds); four
production points per node would roughly halve the cached grid's errors. $10^9$ sampled muons
scatter by $0.8\%$ in $Y_0$ and $2\%$ above $300$\,GeV, and $10^8$ by $3\%$ in $Y$, $4\%$ in the
gradient and $1\%$ in the Hessian. With on-axis muons (the first analysis) the grid was exact to
$10^{-5}$, and quasi-Monte Carlo helped sampling only by a factor two. Importance-sampling the
simulator at a raised temperature does not help either: the hits that matter are at intermediate
momenta, of which a hotter sample holds fewer.

\section{How the surrogate was chosen}
\label{app:study}

The choices come from a study of the surrogates (\texttt{experiments/}) made for the first
analysis (43 parameters, uniform fields, muons on axis) and not repeated here: this analysis uses
the settings it found best. The study used four toy problems with exact answers (a binary outcome
per sample with known probability; 10--43 parameters) and the shield against its reference
designs (Fig.~\ref{fig:study}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Modelling each muon is what makes it work.} A response surface fitted to the same
        simulations without the muons needs about ten times more simulation for the same gradient.
  \item \textbf{Training data.} Many designs with few muons each beat few designs with many; all
        designs at once beat a rolling buffer; the designs must cover the region where the
        derivatives are read. A hotter momentum temperature ($T=2$) is much better than $T=1$ or
        $1.5$, and $T=3$ no better than $T=2$. A tailored training proposal (the provably best one
        for the error on $Y$) would give at most $1.7\times$ the effective training muons of
        $T=2$, so the temperature is kept. Adding designs far from the nominal one helped only
        with an adaptive pool: a pool uniform in the box alone did not lower the box error on the
        nominal muons, with the adaptive pool it fell by a fifth (four seeds each, scored on the
        same references). This analysis therefore uses many $T=2$ designs with $10^6$ muons each,
        a wider pool instead of the uniform one, and an adaptive pool.
  \item \textbf{Architecture.} The DeepONet is the most reliable for the hit rate. At the final
        scale ($2\times10^8$ rows) capacity matters (128 units, 32 features), and averaging seeds
        removes most of the scatter of the gradient. Reduced Taylor surrogates (a per-muon
        quadratic on a low-dimensional subspace), the best Hessians on the toys, are worse here
        (one-at-a-time secants $6$--$10\%$, curvatures $18$--$25\%$). An MLP on $[u,x]$ predicts
        somewhat better (box $11\%$ instead of $16\%$, curvatures $10\%$ instead of $12\%$) and
        agrees with the DeepONet's gradient, top eigenvector and worst direction to
        $\cos=0.99$, so the conclusions do not depend on the choice; the DeepONet is kept because
        its separable form allows cached trunk features and exact derivatives.
\end{itemize}
